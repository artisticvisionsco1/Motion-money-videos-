Losing a hundred dollars hurts about twice as much as winning a hundred feels good. Most people think they make money decisions with logic. But in the late nineteen seventies, psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that losses weigh roughly twice as much as equal gains. They called it loss aversion. It's why people hold losing stocks for years, just to break even. Why they keep all their cash in savings while inflation eats it. And why they skip good opportunities, only because a loss is possible. Of course, caution can protect you. But a fear twice the size of the risk isn't protection. So before you say no, ask yourself: am I avoiding the loss, or just the feeling of it? Because losing a hundred dollars hurts twice as much. That doesn't make it twice as likely.
