The worst option on the menu was put there to make you spend more. Most people think they choose what's best for them. But in one famous experiment, behavioral economist Dan Ariely showed students three Economist subscriptions. Online only, fifty-nine dollars. Print only, one hundred twenty-five. Print and online, also one hundred twenty-five. Nobody picked print only. Eighty-four percent picked the bundle. Then he removed the option nobody wanted. Suddenly, sixty-eight percent chose the cheap online plan. Same prices. Same students. One useless option raised the revenue by about forty-three percent. It was never meant to be bought. It was there to make the expensive choice look like a deal. This is called the decoy effect. Your brain is bad at judging value on its own, so it judges by comparison, and companies design those comparisons for you. The medium popcorn that costs almost as much as the large. The phone that costs a hundred dollars more for a little extra storage. Of course, sometimes the bundle really is the better deal. So before you buy, cover the middle option and ask: would I still choose this? Because the worst option on the menu isn't there for you to buy. It's there to choose for you.
