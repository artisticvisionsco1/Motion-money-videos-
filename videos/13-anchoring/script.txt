The first number you see decides how much you'll pay. Most people believe they judge prices on their own. But in nineteen seventy-four, psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman spun a wheel of fortune in front of people. It was rigged to stop on either ten or sixty-five. Then they asked: what percentage of African countries are in the United Nations? People who saw ten guessed about twenty-five percent. People who saw sixty-five guessed about forty-five. A random number moved their answer by twenty points. This is called anchoring, and businesses use it everywhere. The original price crossed out next to the sale price. The expensive bottle at the top of the wine list. The first salary number in a negotiation. Of course, a high anchor isn't always a trick. Sometimes it's just information. But before you decide if something is a good deal, ignore the number they showed you first, and ask: what would I pay if I had never seen it? Because the first number you see decides how much you'll pay, unless you decide to notice it.
